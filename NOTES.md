# NOTES — One Line, One Laugh: Roast Your Fridge

## The game

The house judge (z-ai/glm-5.3-flash) compares two roast lines at a time and picks
the funnier, weighing IN ORDER:

1. It roasts a fridge or a phone in a single line.
2. It lands a real laugh on a **specific, recognisable detail** of the appliance,
   not a generic insult.
3. It turns on a twist, surprise or double meaning that pays off in the
   **last few words**.
4. It reads cleanly aloud as one line, with no setup needing a second sentence.
5. It feels fresh, not a joke every fridge owner has already worn out.

A difference on an earlier criterion beats any later one. All 15 candidates below
are original, written for this entry; none copies a known joke verbatim and none
copies any house anchor/rung text. Every candidate is one sentence, one line,
84–99 characters — well under the 200-character cap.

## Shortlist (15 candidates)

### Fridge lines

**F1 — ice maker (90 chars)**
> My fridge's ice maker sounds like it's chewing gravel, then proudly serves me three cubes.

- (1) Roasts a fridge, in one line — yes, the ice maker is unmistakably fridge.
- (2) Two concrete, recognisable details at once: the gravel-crunching racket every
  ice maker makes, and the insultingly small yield of exactly three cubes.
- (3) The twist pays off in the last two words: "proudly serves me" sets up a
  triumph, "three cubes" delivers a tiny, bathetic letdown.
- (4) Reads cleanly aloud in one breath, one sentence, no setup left hanging.
- (5) Fresh: ice makers get mocked for noise, but the proud-three-cubes letdown is
  not a worn fridge joke.

**F2 — magnets (94 chars)**
> My fridge has more magnets than food, which explains why nothing inside it ever sticks around.

- (1) Roasts a fridge in one line — yes, the magnet-covered door is pure fridge.
- (2) One specific, instantly recognisable detail: a fridge door buried in magnets.
- (3) The twist is a true double meaning landing on the last two words — "sticks
  around" means both "stays put" and "sticks, like a magnet".
- (4) Reads cleanly aloud, one sentence, no extra setup.
- (5) Fresh: magnets are a fridge staple, but the magnets-vs-food count is not a
  joke everyone has heard.

**F3 — door beep (95 chars)**
> My fridge beeps when the door stays open, like it's the only one in this house with boundaries.

- (1) Roasts a fridge in one line — yes, the door-open beep is fridge-specific.
- (2) One specific detail everyone knows: the nagging beep when the door is left open.
- (3) The twist lands on the very last word — the beep is reframed as the fridge
  having "boundaries", a human trait.
- (4) Reads cleanly aloud, one sentence.
- (5) Weakest of the top three here: "boundaries" therapy-speak is a familiar,
  much-used joke frame, so it feels slightly less fresh than F1 or F2.

**F4 — the light (94 chars)**
> My fridge light only works when the door is open, so even it refuses to witness what's inside.

- (1) Roasts a fridge in one line — yes.
- (2) Specific and recognisable: the fridge light that is only ever on with the door open.
- (3) The twist lands in the last few words — the light itself "refuses to witness"
  the contents, as if the inside were too horrifying to look at.
- (4) Reads cleanly aloud, one sentence.
- (5) Loses freshness: "what's really in the fridge" / the light going out when the
  door closes is one of the oldest fridge jokes there is.

**F5 — crisper drawer (87 chars)**

- (1) Roasts a fridge in one line — yes, the crisper drawer is fridge-specific.

- (3) The twist lands in the last few words — vegetables "confessing" turns rot into
  a courtroom surrender.
- (4) Reads cleanly aloud, one sentence.
- (5) Middling freshness: dead-vegetables-in-the-crisper is a well-worn fridge trope,
  even with the confession twist.

**F6 — the 2019 jar (98 chars)**

**F7 — the hum (91 chars)**

## Winner, by ordered comparison

**Winner: F1 — "My fridge's ice maker sounds like it's chewing gravel, then proudly
serves me three cubes." (90 chars)**

All fifteen lines pass criterion (1): each roasts a fridge or a phone in one line.
The decision is made at criterion (2). F1 carries **two** concrete, sensory,
recognisable details where every rival carries one: the gravel-crunching noise
every ice maker makes, plus the exact, insulting yield of three cubes. F2's
magnets and F3's beep are each a single visual or sound detail; P3's autocorrect
and P7's cracked screen are each a single behaviour. On criterion (3) F1 is at
least level with the best: "three cubes" pays off in the final two words, flipping
the proud buildup ("proudly serves me") into a tiny letdown — and F2's double
meaning ("sticks around"), while elegant, is not *much* stronger than F1's
letdown, so under the judge's own rule ("let the first differing criterion decide
unless a later one differs by much more") the (2) advantage stands. F1 also wins
criterion (5) over the other finalists: the proud-three-cubes letdown is not a
joke anyone has heard before, where F3's "boundaries" and P7's cracked screen are
familiar frames. F1 reads cleanly aloud (4), wastes no word, and at 90 characters
sits far under the 200 cap.

Runner-up: F2 (magnets) — the best double meaning in the list, losing only on the
single-detail-vs-two-detail comparison at criterion (2).
Third: F3 (beep) — a clean twist on a specific sound, losing on freshness at (5).

## Read-aloud test (top 3)

Read aloud, each in one breath:

- **F1:** twist lands on the last two words ("three cubes"); the gravel sound and
  the three-cube yield are both specific and instantly recognisable; no word is
  wasted ("proudly" does the setup work, "three" does the punch work); 90 chars.
- **F2:** twist lands on the last two words ("sticks around"); the magnet-covered
  door is specific; "which explains why" is the only slightly slack phrase — it
  costs F2 nothing on (4) but adds no laugh.
- **F3:** twist lands on the last word ("boundaries"); the beep is specific;
  "the only one in this house" is tight; the therapy-speak frame is the only
  freshness risk.
